## Browsing by colour

The project began with photographs on my bedroom wall. While arranging my
prints, I found that grouping similar colours gave the collection a pleasing visual
structure. Complementary colours helped neighbouring groups meet, creating
connections between photographs of different subjects.

I wanted to explore that arrangement digitally. A graph lets each photograph
connect to several others, so viewers can follow relationships through the
collection instead of choosing a category first. The implementation currently
models similar-colour connections; the complementary relationships on the wall
remain outside its comparison model.

Building it involved three decisions: how to represent a photograph's colour,
which comparisons should become links, and how to arrange those links while
keeping the photographs readable.

## Defining what “similar” means

I started with a simple descriptor: **the average RGB colour of each photograph,
converted into a CIELAB vector**. The average provides a broad signal about the
image's colour cast and illumination. It is also easy to inspect: I can display
the descriptor as a swatch beside the photograph and see what the reduction has
preserved.

The processing order matters. I average the RGB channels across the analysis
image, then convert that average into LAB. This produces three coordinates,

$[L^*, a^*, b^*]$: lightness, a green-to-red axis and a blue-to-yellow
axis.

For photograph Iᵢ, the representation and comparison are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{v}_i &= \operatorname{Lab}\!\left(\operatorname{meanRGB}(I_i)\right) \\
d_{ij} &= \Delta E_{00}(\mathbf{v}_i, \mathbf{v}_j)
\end{aligned}
$$

**CIEDE2000** adjusts differences in lightness, chroma and hue to account for
perceptual unevenness in LAB. Chroma describes how far a colour is from neutral;
hue describes its colour direction. The formula includes a chroma–hue interaction,
so its result is more involved than the straight-line distance between the three
coordinates.

The descriptor makes this comparison compact, but also limits what it can
recognise. Separate blue and magenta regions in a performance photograph collapse
into one average. Different colour distributions can produce similar vectors,
and the positions of objects and colours disappear entirely. The graph therefore
follows overall colour affinity, with no knowledge of what a photograph depicts.

## Turning similarity into a useful graph

With 100 photographs, comparing every unique pair requires **4,950 calculations**.
That is manageable during preprocessing, but drawing every pair would overwhelm
the images. I select a sparse set of relationships:

1. Calculate colour differences between photographs.
2. Exclude candidates beyond the colour-difference cutoff.
3. Keep up to four nearest neighbours for each photograph.
4. Combine those directional selections into undirected links.
5. Convert the retained differences into weights for the layout.

The neighbour limit applies to each photograph's selections. Its final number
of links can be higher because other photographs can select it too. A connection
therefore survives when either end chooses the other.

For colour difference d and cutoff τ, I calculate the link weight as:

$$
s = \max\!\left(0,\min\!\left(1,1-\frac{d}{\tau}\right)\right)
$$

Closer matches receive larger weights. At the cutoff, the weight reaches zero
and no link is retained. The cutoff therefore affects both which relationships
appear and the weights of the surviving links.

Increasing the neighbour limit gives photographs more connections, but adds
clutter. Tightening the cutoff can leave photographs isolated. I used the manual
colour review alongside visual inspection of the graph to settle on the current
defaults: **up to four neighbours and a CIEDE2000 cutoff of 16**.

## Making the graph usable

Image analysis and neighbour generation happen when the archive is updated.
The browsing interface loads the precomputed graph, so panning and zooming do
not require decoding original images or comparing photographs again.

A force layout lets a photograph be drawn toward several related images at
once. Stronger link weights favour shorter distances and stronger attraction;
repulsion spreads the collection out. The resulting positions reflect those
competing influences across the graph.

Photographs occupy rectangles rather than points. I preserve their aspect ratios
and use rectangle collisions to account for their different dimensions.
Preferred link spacing also considers image size, reducing overlap between
large photographs.

Rendering is handled separately from the simulation. Images load near the
viewport and upgrade resolution as zoom and device pixel ratio require it.
The canvas follows its container size, and narrow screens retain a pannable
view rather than fitting the whole collection into tiny thumbnails.

The layout is a way to explore the relationships. Moving a photograph changes
its screen coordinates while its colour vector and calculated differences stay
the same. Nearby images may also be positioned together because of their other
neighbours, so screen distance is only a visual expression of the graph's
relationships.

## Updating the archive

Adding a photograph can change the nearest neighbours of existing photographs
as well as introducing a new node. The admin pipeline prepares image features,
uploads the original and generates the affected relationships. Cloudflare R2
delivers published images; Supabase stores metadata and graph state.

One failure I needed to handle was a committed upload whose response never
reaches the browser. Repeating an ordinary insert could create duplicates.
Instead, each batch has a stable identity: retrying a completed batch returns
its recorded result, while reusing that identity with a different payload is
rejected.

A separate revision check handles concurrent updates. If the graph changes
while relationships are being generated, registration rejects the stale work
so it can be regenerated. New nodes, affected relationships and the accepted
batch result are committed together in a database transaction.

The average-colour descriptor remains the clearest limitation. It can connect
photographs with different distributions of colour and separate photographs
whose lighting differs, even when their subjects are closely related. My next
experiment would use a held-out review focused on those cases, testing whether
a distribution-aware representation produces more useful browsing paths. That
would give the added complexity a concrete purpose: preserving visual
relationships that the current average cannot express.
