## Translating Allie's work into a website

My friend Allie creates real-time, audio-reactive lighting for events. She wanted a
site that responded like her shows: an environment visitors could affect, rather
than a static collection of clips. Her work inspired a response driven by video
brightness, pointer movement and clicks.

Allie chose the typeface and supplied visual references. I designed and developed
the interface, Three.js scenes and media pipeline around that starting point.

## Building the reactive scene

I used Three.js through React Three Fiber to give the background real depth. The
video plays on a subdivided plane behind the wordmark. A [vertex shader](https://github.com/TheTexta/bur1alrites/blob/33591fd36f586567a7d7ac058bf8bac9047d73a5/src/app/room.tsx#L309-L363)
converts the video's colour samples into linear-light luminance, then smooths
neighbouring samples so fine detail does not turn into a jagged surface. That
smoothed brightness pushes the mesh forward: highlights lift while dark areas
stay flat.

Pointer input modifies that same relief. I raycast the pointer onto the video
plane to locate the interaction within the image, then ease in a soft, local
amplification around it. A click sends an expanding, fading ring out from that
point. Both effects multiply the brightness-driven displacement rather than
adding an unrelated distortion, so the footage still determines the surface's
shape. The camera also drifts with the pointer, creating parallax between the
background, floating wordmark and foreground objects.

## Presenting video spatially

The desktop gallery introduces spatial depth before a visitor enters the projector
room. Its preview planes sit in front of the background, so camera movement
produces parallax between them. Inverted previews reveal their colour and play on
hover or keyboard focus; choosing a clip opens that video in the room.

The room makes the footage a source of light within a space, echoing how Allie's
lighting changes the environment around it. Unlike the reactive background, its
screen stays flat. I downsample video frames to an 8 × 8 image and average their
colour and luminance, then ease the room light towards those values. The floor
reflects the screen, while boosted highlights and bloom let its glow extend beyond
the image. On desktop, visitors can also click to look around and use WASD to move
through the room.

The supplied footage was heavily compressed. I used animated grain to make the
artifacts less conspicuous and give the projection a texture that suited the
material. The noise changes over time inside the screen's material shader,
working with the source rather than presenting it as a pristine digital screen.

On mobile, visitors browse the same archive in a responsive grid. Tapping a clip
plays or pauses it inline, revealing its colour; starting another stops the
previous preview. Gallery taps keep visitors on the page rather than opening the
projector room. The reactive opening scene remains available when WebGL and the
visitor's motion preference allow it.

## Managing video and rendering costs

Video decoding and the 3D scene compete for browser resources. A viewport pool
preloads previews in and just outside the viewport at the lowest HLS rendition,
with a short buffer. On desktop, the active preview is the hovered or
keyboard-focused card: only that gallery video plays and uploads frames to its
scene texture. Inactive previews stop buffering after their first frame is ready,
and scrolling cards out of the preload area releases their streams and video
resources. Opening the projector room also pauses the background video and freezes
the covered scene's render loop.

Rapid scrolling creates an ownership problem: a stream can finish attaching after
its card has left and returned with a new player. The [viewport video pool](https://github.com/TheTexta/bur1alrites/blob/33591fd36f586567a7d7ac058bf8bac9047d73a5/src/lib/viewport-video-pool.ts)
checks instance identity before accepting that completion and destroys stale
controllers. Its [lifecycle regression test](https://github.com/TheTexta/bur1alrites/blob/33591fd36f586567a7d7ac058bf8bac9047d73a5/src/lib/viewport-video-pool.test.ts)
recreates that sequence and checks that the old controller is destroyed while the
replacement remains alive.

### Playback lifecycle checks

Recovery is bounded: an active preview gets at most two delayed automatic retries,
with a stalled stream replaced if playback stops progressing. Leaving the preload
area or hiding the page cancels pending work. Autoplay denial does not start a
retry loop, and posters stay visible until playback presents a frame. The same
pool handles inline mobile previews; tests also cover rapid leave and re-entry
while a play promise is pending.

### Adaptive scene quality

A separate [quality controller](https://github.com/TheTexta/bur1alrites/blob/33591fd36f586567a7d7ac058bf8bac9047d73a5/src/app/scene-quality-controller.ts)
responds to sustained frame times by reducing pixel ratio, reflection resolution
and bloom work. It waits longer before upgrading than downgrading: a brief
improvement should not immediately raise quality and trigger another slowdown.
The lowest preset also stops video-colour sampling for the room light, retaining
a dim baseline instead.

The linked source and tests are pinned to the October implementation. Mocked
playback and [synthetic-frame tests](https://github.com/TheTexta/bur1alrites/blob/33591fd36f586567a7d7ac058bf8bac9047d73a5/src/app/scene-quality-controller.test.ts)
check lifecycle and quality policies, not real-device codec support or GPU
performance.

## Publishing and maintaining the work

Next.js keeps public pages and admin routes in one app. Allie can upload MOV or MP4
masters through multipart transfers to R2. A separate FFmpeg worker prepares HLS
playback renditions and posters while preserving the masters, so she does not
have to prepare browser-ready versions by hand. Failed parts and finalization can
be retried while the page stays open; upload sessions are not restored after a
reload.

This was my first Three.js project. The central lesson was that the scene and its
media players could not be designed separately: deciding which video owned a
texture, when it could play and when to release it became as important as building
the scene itself.
